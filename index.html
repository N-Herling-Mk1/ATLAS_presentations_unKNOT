Is our background one distribution, or several mixed together?

We search for multiple background distributions 4 ways, using background data only:

  1. 1
    Count themFit the background as a blend of 1 to 4 groups; three independent judges must agree on the number.
  2. 2
    Trend or real groups?Pit one background with a smooth internal trend against separate groups, at fixed detector activity.
  3. 3
    Where they pointCompare vertex directions with where the detector can actually see decays.
  4. 4
    What the momentum saysCollision debris points the missing momentum back at its vertex; other sources do not.

What the blind test shows

Reached independently: 3 matches

  1. Backbone: the background is a sum of sources that each factorise
  2. Mixing sources creates the score correlation that breaks ABCD (her covariance formula; our sign flip)
  3. With two variables only the total is pinned down, not each source's shape or weight

Where UNKNOT differs: 5 choices

  1. K is inferred with stated confidence, not fixed
  2. Factorisation inside a source is tested, not assumed
  3. Physics features, not the two network scores
  4. No hidden signal region: all of the background is used
  5. Origin checks: direction against the detector map, missing-momentum alignment
Not done yet: UNKNOT has not been run in rank-K's own space, the two network scores. That run is the direct cross-check.

The bottom line, before any detail

Endcap: one background, not several

What looked like separate groups is one background whose shape shifts smoothly with how busy the detector is.

Barrel: two groups that hold up

Two groups survive every check we have. One sits at the barrel's outer edge. We don't yet know their origin.

Direction gives the best clues

Quiet barrel vertices lean vertical (like cosmic rays). Busy ones lean horizontal (like beam halo).

Two things the search must fix

Mixing groups fakes the score correlation our background method forbids, and one endcap score is reacting to a placeholder value.

1. Check the data

Fit the data as a blend of 1 to 4 groups and let independent tests pick the count.

4. Check groups against physics

Compare the groups on jets, pile-up, direction and run-by-run stability.

The statistics behind the four ways

FOUNDATION

A smooth curve for each measurement

Maximum entropy: the least-assuming curve that matches the measured averages. No histogram bins.

WAY 1

The background as a blend of K sources

Inside each source the measurements are independent; correlations come only from blending.

WAY 1

Choosing K

A scorecard penalises extra sources; a shuffle test asks whether K+1 beats what scrambled real events can do.

WAY 2

One source with a smooth trend

The rival to “more sources”. Both models are scored on events they never saw.

WAY 3

Directions against the detector map

How far the background's share of up, sideways and vertical vertices sits from the simulated-decay map.

WAY 4

Missing momentum pointing back

Fit the share of events aligned with their vertex; a group that aligns less holds extra non-collision events.

Most of what we see is not the particle we are looking for

The search

A rare, long-lived particle could decay inside the outer muon detector and leave a tight cluster of hits, a “vertex”.

The catch

Almost every vertex we record is background: something ordinary that only looks like the real thing.

The question

How many different kinds of background are mixed together, and which is which?

Three suspects

Collision debris (“punch-through”)

Pieces of ordinary particle sprays that leak all the way out to the muon detector.

Beam halo

Particles travelling alongside the proton beams, outside the collisions themselves.

Cosmic rays

Muons from space that pass through the detector from above.

We only believe a count when independent judges agree

Scorecard

Rewards a better fit, penalises extra groups. Two versions; one is strict about overlapping groups.

Re-draw the data

Repeat on 30 random halves of the data. A real count comes back almost every time.

Shuffle test

Scramble the data so no groups can exist, and see if the real data still beat the scramble.

Stress test 1

The count must hold as the smooth curves are given more freedom.

Stress test 2

The count must repeat in two independent halves of the data-taking runs.

The trap: a smooth trend can pretend to be two groups

An everyday version

Shoe size rises smoothly with age. Fit “two groups” to a school's shoe sizes and you will find “kids” and “teens”, even though nobody belongs to a separate species.

Endcap oddities belong to everyone

  • More vertices in the upper half than the detector map predicts (65% vs 54%), and more near horizontal (34% vs 25%).
  • A repeating 8-fold pattern around the detector: 42% of vertices sit in a slice that should hold 25%.
  • Quiet and busy vertices show the same pattern, so it is not the mark of a separate source.

Mixing groups can fake the link our method forbids

Why we care

Our background estimate (ABCD) needs the two network scores to be unrelated. Inside each group they lean one way; pooled, the sign flips.

Barrel too: almost all of the score link lives in the outer-edge group (+18.5) versus +2.0 in the central one. Knowing the groups points at where the estimate can break.
